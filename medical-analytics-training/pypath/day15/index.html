🤔 Why this matters

One of the most famous beginner datasets in data science is the passenger list of the Titanic, the ship that sank in 1912. Who survived, and why? Today we work on a dataset inspired by it: the passengers of the Northern Star, a ship that never existed. Every passenger and number below is made up by a few lines of Python, so the results are about our invented ship, not about real people.

A mini-project is like a recipe that uses everything in your kitchen. You already know each step (pandas, cleaning, grouping, charts, EDA); today you see how they fit together into one story. On Day 22 you'll come back to this same ship and teach a computer to predict who survives.

Remember: the code boxes on this page share their variables, like pages of one notebook. Run them from top to bottom. The first box loads pandas and can take a few seconds.

❓ Step 1: Questions first

Good analysis starts with questions, not with code. Here are ours for the Northern Star:

  1. How many passengers survived overall?
  2. Did women and men survive at different rates?
  3. Did first-class passengers (the most expensive tickets) do better?
  4. Were children more likely to survive?
  5. Which group did best, and which did worst?

📊 Step 5: Survival rates

Here's a neat trick. survived holds only 0s and 1s, so its mean is the share of 1s. For example, the mean of [1, 0, 1, 1] is 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75, which means 75% survived. That share is called the survival rate.

📝 Step 7: Findings

The last step is to answer the questions in plain sentences that someone who never saw the code can understand. Building them from the variables means they stay correct if the data changes:

Shift + Enter

table.stack() turns the pivot table into one long column whose labels are (sex, class) pairs, and idxmax() gives the label of the biggest value (like argmax from Day 9, but it returns the name instead of the position).

Careful with "why": the data shows that women and first-class passengers survived more often. It doesn't prove why (Day 14: correlation is not causation). In our made-up ship we know the reason, because we wrote it into the chance line of the first box. In real data you never get to read that line.

⚠️ Common mistakes

  • Calculating before cleaning: duplicated rows count some passengers twice.
  • Forgetting that the mean of a 0/1 column is a share (0.41), not a count. Multiply by 100 or use :.0% for a percentage.
  • Misspelling a column ("class" instead of "pclass") → KeyError.
  • Trusting a rate from a tiny group (4 seniors) as much as one from 259 adults.
  • Saying one thing causes another just because they go together.

✅ Recap

  • A mini-project follows the EDA routine: questions → inspect → clean → calculate → chart → findings.
  • drop_duplicates() and fillna(median) fix the two classic glitches.
  • The mean of a 0/1 column is a rate; groupby(...)["survived"].mean() gives a rate per group.
  • pd.cut(column, bins=..., labels=...) sorts numbers into named ranges.
  • pivot_table compares two columns at once; idxmax() finds the label of the biggest value.
  • Findings are plain sentences, built from variables, and careful about cause and effect.
