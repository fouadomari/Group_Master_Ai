Decision trees and random forests

🎯 Today's goal

By the end of this hour you will:

  • understand a decision tree as a game of "20 questions" about the data
  • train one with DecisionTreeClassifier and control its size with max_depth
  • read the rules a tree learned, as text and as a picture
  • spot overfitting by comparing train and test accuracy
  • build a random forest (many trees voting) and see which features matter most

🤔 Why this matters

Think of the game "20 questions". Your friend thinks of an animal, and you ask yes/no questions: "Does it fly?" No. "Does it live in water?" Yes. "Is it bigger than a person?" Yes… "A whale!" Good players ask the questions that split the possibilities best.

A decision tree plays exactly this game with data. It learns a list of questions like "Is the colour intensity at most 3.8?" and follows the answers down to a prediction. Trees are popular in banks, hospitals and shops because a person can read the rules and check them. A random forest is a crowd of trees that vote, and it is one of the most used models for table-shaped data.

🤖 Training a decision tree

Same pattern as every scikit-learn model (Day 16): split the data, create the model, fit, then score on the test set. max_depth=2 means "ask at most 2 questions in a row", which keeps the tree tiny and easy to read.

Shift + Enter

Only two questions in a row, and it already gets most of the test wines right. How does the tree choose a question? It tries every column and many cut-off values, and keeps the question that leaves the two groups least mixed (ideally each group holds just one grower). scikit-learn measures "mixed" with a number called gini: 0 means "all one class", bigger means more mixed.

📈 Deeper trees and overfitting

What if we let the tree ask more questions? Let's try several depths in a loop (Day 5) and write down train and test accuracy each time. None means "no limit: keep asking until every leaf is pure" (holds wines from only one grower).

Shift + Enter

Look at the train column: from depth 4 on, the tree scores a perfect 1.000 on the training wines. It has asked enough questions to give every single training wine its own little corner. But the test score doesn't follow; it stays below 1. That is overfitting: the model memorised the training examples, including their random quirks, instead of learning the general pattern. It's like a student who learns last year's exam answers by heart and then meets new questions.

Rule of thumb: a big gap between train and test accuracy is the warning sign of overfitting. A perfect train score is not a reason to celebrate. Limiting max_depth is the simplest cure. On this small, clean dataset the gap stays modest; on messy real data an unlimited tree can do far worse on the test set.

A single tree has another weakness: it is jumpy. Train it on a slightly different split and you can get quite different questions and scores. Let's split the data 5 different ways:

🌲🌲🌲 Random forests: many trees vote

Ask one person to guess the number of sweets in a jar and they may be far off. Ask 100 people and take the average, and the guess is usually much better: the individual mistakes cancel out. A random forest uses this "wisdom of the crowd":

  1. It trains many trees (n_estimators of them, for example 100).
  2. Each tree sees a slightly different random sample of the training rows, and at every question it may only pick from a random handful of the columns. So the trees end up different from each other.
  3. To predict, every tree votes, and the class with the most votes wins.
Shift + Enter

random_state=42 fixes the random choices, so you get the same forest every time you run the box. Now the same 5-splits experiment, single tree versus forest:

Shift + Enter

The forest is better on every split, and its scores jump around less. The price: you can't read 100 trees the way you read one. You give up some explainability for accuracy.

A forest still tells you which columns mattered, averaged over all its trees. This list is usually more trustworthy than the one from a single tree:

Shift + Enter

Careful: importance says what the model used, not what causes the result (remember "correlation is not causation" from Day 14). When two columns carry similar information, the forest may split the credit between them.

⚠️ Common mistakes

  • Calling .predict() before .fit() → NotFittedError.
  • Celebrating 100% train accuracy. Always look at the test score, and at the gap between them.
  • Letting a tree grow without limit on small data. Try a few max_depth values and compare test scores.
  • Forgetting feature_names=list(X.columns) in export_text or plot_tree, and getting rules about feature_0, feature_1…
  • Forgetting random_state=42: your forest (and its scores) change a little every run.
  • Reading feature importance as "this causes that".

✅ Recap

  • A decision tree learns a chain of yes/no questions; each leaf gives a prediction.
  • DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=…, random_state=42), then fit, predict, score.
  • export_text(tree, feature_names=…) and plot_tree(...) show the learned rules.
  • Train accuracy far above test accuracy = overfitting. Smaller depth helps.
  • RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=100, random_state=42): many different trees vote; usually more accurate and steadier than one tree.
  • feature_importances_ shows which columns the model relied on (not causes).
