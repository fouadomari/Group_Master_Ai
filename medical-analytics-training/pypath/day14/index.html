🤔 Why this matters

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) means getting to know a dataset before you trust it or build anything on it. It's like a doctor's first examination: look, measure, compare, notice anything unusual, and only then decide what to do.

Every real data project starts this way, and so will the machine-learning days ahead. EDA uses everything from the last five days: pandas for loading, filtering and grouping, cleaning for the problems you find, and charts to see patterns. Today you'll put it all together into one routine you can reuse on any dataset.

Remember: the code boxes on this page share their variables, like pages of one notebook. Run them from top to bottom. The first box loads NumPy and pandas, which can take a few seconds.

🧭 The EDA routine

  1. Questions first: what do you want to find out?
  2. Shape and types: how many rows and columns, and what kind is each column?
  3. Missing values: where are the gaps?
  4. Distributions: how is each column spread out?
  5. Groups: do categories differ from each other?
  6. Relationships: do two columns move together?
  7. Outliers: are there values that don't fit?
  8. Findings: write down what you learned, in plain words.

📶 Step 4: distributions

A distribution is how the values of a column are spread out: where most of them are, how far they reach, and whether some are far away from the rest. describe() sums it up in numbers:

Shift + Enter

How to read it, row by row:

  • count: filled cells (sleep has only 191).
  • mean: the average. std (standard deviation): how far values typically are from the mean.
  • min / max: the smallest and largest value. Look here first for strange values!
  • 25%, 50%, 75%: the quartiles. A quarter of the students are below the 25% value, half are below the 50% value (the median), three quarters are below the 75% value.

Did you spot it? Most students study 7 to 12 hours a week, but the max is 45. That's suspicious. Keep it in mind. A histogram shows the shapes:

Scores form a hill around 60. Study hours form a hill around 9, plus a few lonely bars far to the right: those are the strange values. For a text column, the "distribution" is simply how often each value appears. normalize=True gives shares (fractions of 1) instead of counts:

So about 40% of the students are from North, 36% from South and 25% from East.

🔗 Step 6: relationships and correlation

Do students who study more score higher? The correlation, written r, measures how strongly two number columns move together in a straight-line way. It is always between −1 and +1:

rMeaning
close to +1when one goes up, the other goes up too (strong)
around +0.3 to +0.7they tend to go up together, with plenty of exceptions
close to 0no straight-line relationship
negativewhen one goes up, the other tends to go down

Correlation only works on numbers, so calling it on the whole table fails:

Shift + Enter

"could not convert string to float: 'East'": pandas tried to turn the school names into numbers. Pick the number columns first. For one pair of columns use column_a.corr(column_b):

Shift + Enter

Read the table by finding a row and a column: where score meets study_hours you find their r. (The diagonal is always 1: every column matches itself perfectly.) Study hours have the strongest link with the score, about 0.35. A scatter plot shows what's behind that number:

Shift + Enter

A clear upward cloud on the left, and three lonely dots far to the right. Those three points pull the line and make r smaller than it should be.

Correlation is not causation. Even a strong r only says two things go together, not that one causes the other. Ice-cream sales and sunburns rise together, but ice cream doesn't cause sunburn: hot sunny weather causes both. Maybe students who study more are also more motivated, and motivation raises the score. Data like ours can't tell the difference.

🚩 Step 7: outliers

An outlier is a value far away from the rest. Some are mistakes (a typo of 38 instead of 3.8), some are real but rare. A common rule to flag them uses the IQR (interquartile range): the distance between the 25% value (Q1) and the 75% value (Q3), where the middle half of the data lives. Anything more than 1.5 × IQR below Q1 or above Q3 is flagged. quantile(0.25) gives Q1:

Shift + Enter

Four students are flagged. 38, 42.5 and 45 hours a week of study, with fairly low scores, look like typing mistakes. But 19.2 hours is possible for a hard-working student. The rule only flags candidates; you decide what to do with each. Let's see how much the three mistakes were hiding:

Shift + Enter

Three wrong values out of 200 almost halved the correlation. That's why outliers deserve attention before you draw any conclusion.

⚠️ Common mistakes

  • Jumping into charts with no question in mind. Write your questions first.
  • Calling .corr() on a table with text columns → ValueError: could not convert string to float. Select the number columns.
  • Reading correlation as cause: "goes together with" is all r can say.
  • Deleting every flagged outlier automatically. Check each one: is it a mistake or a rare but real value?
  • Trusting small differences between groups. A few points with small groups may just be luck.

✅ Recap

  • The EDA routine: questions → shape/types → missing → distributions → groups → relationships → outliers → findings.
  • describe() gives count, mean, std, min, quartiles and max; check min and max for odd values.
  • value_counts(normalize=True) gives shares; pd.crosstab(a, b, normalize="index") compares two categories.
  • df[cols].corr() and a.corr(b) give r between −1 and +1. Correlation is not causation.
  • IQR rule: iqr = q3 - q1; flag values below q1 - 1.5*iqr or above q3 + 1.5*iqr.
  • Finish with a few plain-English findings, numbers included.
