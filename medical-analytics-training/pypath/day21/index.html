Clustering with K-means

🤔 Why this matters

Pour a big bag of mixed sweets on a table and ask a friend to sort them. Nobody tells them the "right" groups, yet they will make piles: by colour, by size, by wrapper. They put similar things together.

Since Day 16 every model learned from examples with the right answer (the label y). That is supervised learning. Often there is no label at all: a shop has thousands of customers but nobody has written "bargain hunter" or "big spender" next to them. Clustering finds such groups by itself. This is unsupervised learning. Shops use it for customer segments, biologists to group cells, and journalists to group similar documents.

👣 How K-means works

K is the number of groups you ask for, and a mean is an average. K-means describes every group by its centre (the average point of the group) and repeats four simple steps:

  1. Pick K starting centres at random.
  2. Assign every point to its nearest centre.
  3. Move each centre to the average of the points assigned to it.
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the centres stop moving.

We can watch it happen. max_iter limits how many rounds of "assign + move" it may do, so we stop it after 1 round, after 3 rounds, and when it is finished. The black crosses are the centres:

Shift + Enter

At first, two centres share one real group and one centre must cover two groups. Round by round the centres slide towards the middle of the real groups. (init="random", n_init=1 and max_iter are only used here to show the steps. Normally you leave them out.)

The usual pattern, but notice: fit gets only X. There is no y, because there are no answers. Afterwards the model holds:

  • labels_: the cluster number of every point (0, 1, 2…)
  • cluster_centers_: the coordinates of each centre
Shift + Enter

New tool: np.bincount(labels) counts how many times each number 0, 1, 2… appears.

The numbers are just names. "Cluster 0" is not better or first in any way. Run with a different random_state and the same groups may get different numbers. It's up to you, the human, to look at each cluster and give it a meaningful name.

📏 How tight are the clusters? Inertia

inertia_ adds up, for every point, the squared distance to its own centre. Small inertia means the points sit close to their centres: tight clusters. K-means always tries to make this number as small as possible.

Because K-means starts from random centres, an unlucky start can get stuck in a poor answer. Let's try 5 single starts:

Shift + Enter

Some starts got stuck with a much bigger inertia (two centres shared one blob). That is why we write n_init=10: K-means runs 10 times from different starts and keeps the run with the smallest inertia. Together with random_state=42, the result is good and the same every time.

💪 How many clusters? The elbow method

With real data you don't know K. Could we just pick the K with the smallest inertia? No: more clusters always means smaller inertia (with one cluster per point it would be 0!). Instead we draw inertia for K = 1, 2, 3… and look for the elbow: the point where adding another cluster stops helping much.

Shift + Enter

From 1 to 2 to 3 clusters the inertia falls steeply; after 3 the line is almost flat. The bend, like an elbow in an arm, says K = 3. Real data rarely has such a sharp elbow, so also ask: "Can I describe and use these groups?"

🛍️ Customer segments, and why to scale first

Now a made-up shop with 120 customers. For each one we know how much they spent last year (in dollars) and how many times a month they visit:

Shift + Enter

f"C{number:03d}" writes a number with 3 digits, padded with zeros: C001, C002…

K-means measures distances, so it only understands numbers. Giving it the whole table, ID text included, fails:

Shift + Enter

"could not convert string to float: 'C001'": Python tried to turn the ID text into a number. Select only the number columns: customers[["spend", "visits"]].

There's a second, sneakier problem. Spend is in the thousands, visits are below 20. A difference of 10 visits (a huge change in behaviour!) counts as much as a difference of 10 dollars (nothing). So K-means looks almost only at spend. Compare the clusters without and with scaling. StandardScaler (Day 18) puts both columns on the same scale: mean 0, standard deviation 1.

Shift + Enter

fit_predict is a shortcut: fit, then give back labels_, in one call.

Without scaling, the low spenders are cut into two halves by spend, ignoring that some of them visit 12 times a month and others twice. After scaling, both columns count, and the three real groups appear. Rule: scale before clustering whenever columns use different units.

Clusters are only useful once you can describe them. Add the labels as a column and compare the groups with groupby (Day 11):

Now a human gives the segments names, for example: "rare visitors" (low spend, about 2 visits), "frequent small shoppers" (low spend, about 12 visits) and "big spenders" (high spend). The shop could send each group a different offer.

🌸 Does clustering find real groups?

On real data we can't check clusters, because there are no answers. But the iris flowers from Day 16 do have answers (the species). So let's cluster them while hiding the species, and only afterwards compare with pd.crosstab (Day 14):

Shift + Enter

Without ever seeing a species name, K-means puts all 50 setosa flowers in one cluster. Versicolor and virginica look more alike, so some of them are mixed up. Clustering finds groups that are similar, which is not always the same as the groups we care about.

⚠️ Common mistakes

  • Giving K-means text columns (like IDs or names) → ValueError: could not convert string to float.
  • Forgetting to scale columns with different units. The biggest numbers win.
  • Thinking cluster numbers mean something. "Cluster 0" is just a name.
  • Choosing K by the smallest inertia. More clusters always lower it; look for the elbow.
  • Leaving out n_init=10 and random_state=42: results can change, or get stuck in a poor answer.
  • Believing clusters are "the truth". They are groups of similar points; a human must check that they make sense.

✅ Recap

  • Unsupervised learning: only X, no y. Clustering puts similar points together.
  • K-means: pick K centres → assign each point to the nearest → move centres to the average → repeat.
  • KMeans(n_clusters=K, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit(X), then labels_, cluster_centers_, inertia_, predict(new).
  • Elbow method: plot inertia for K = 1, 2, 3… and pick the bend.
  • Scale first (StandardScaler), then describe each cluster with groupby(...).mean().
