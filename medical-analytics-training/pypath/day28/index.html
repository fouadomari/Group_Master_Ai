🤔 Why this matters

Yesterday you cleaned text into tokens. But every model from the machine learning days, from linear regression to neural networks, only understands numbers. So we need a way to turn a text into numbers.

The simplest idea: throw all the words of a text into a bag, shake it, and count what's inside. The order is lost ("dog bites man" and "man bites dog" look the same), but the counts already tell a lot about what a text is about. This bag-of-words idea, and its smarter cousin TF-IDF, powered search engines and spam filters for years, and they are still a strong first try for any text problem.

🔗 Word pairs: n-grams

A bag of single words loses the order, so "not good" becomes just not and good. An n-gram is a group of n words in a row: a unigram is one word, a bigram two. With ngram_range=(1, 2) the vectorizer counts both:

Shift + Enter

Now not good is its own column, so a model can learn that it means something different from good. The price: many more columns.

⚖️ TF-IDF: which words really matter?

Plain counts have a weakness. garlic appears in four of our eight recipes, so it doesn't tell recipes apart very well. chocolate appears in only one recipe, so it says a lot about that recipe. TF-IDF gives each word a score that rewards both:

PartStands forQuestion it answers
TFterm frequencyHow often is the word in this document? More often → bigger.
IDFinverse document frequencyIn how many documents does the word appear? In fewer documents → bigger.
TF-IDFTF × IDFIs the word frequent here and rare elsewhere? Then it's important here.

TfidfVectorizer works exactly like CountVectorizer, but gives these scores instead of counts. (It also scales each row so long and short documents can be compared fairly.) Let's look at the IDF part first:

Shift + Enter

garlic (in 4 recipes) gets the smallest IDF, chocolate (in 1) the biggest. Now the full TF-IDF scores of recipe 0, "Tomato pasta with garlic and fresh basil", sorted from most to least important:

Shift + Enter

Each word appears once, but pasta and basil score highest because few other recipes use them; garlic scores lowest. The top TF-IDF words make a good short summary of a document: they are its keywords.

🧭 How similar are two texts?

Now that every recipe is a vector, we can compare them. Cosine similarity compares two vectors and gives a score from 0 (no words in common) to 1 (the same words in the same proportions). The name comes from geometry: it measures the angle between the two vectors, but you don't need the maths to use it. cosine_similarity(T) compares every document with every other one:

Shift + Enter

The diagonal is 1 (every recipe is identical to itself). Recipe 0 (tomato pasta) is most similar to recipe 4 (tomato salad, 0.48): they share tomato, fresh and basil. Recipe 2 (chocolate cake) only resembles recipe 5 (lemon cake).

That's all a basic search engine needs: turn the question (the query) into a vector with the same vectorizer, compare it with every document, and pick the highest score. NumPy's .argmax() (Day 9) gives the position of the biggest number:

Shift + Enter

{docs[best]!r} shows the text with quotes around it. Try your own queries, like "a sweet cake" or "pasta with cheese".

⚠️ Common mistakes

  • Passing one string instead of a list: transform("text") → ValueError. Use transform(["text"]).
  • Calling fit or fit_transform again on the query. Fit once on the documents, then only transform new texts, or the columns won't match.
  • Expecting new words to be counted: words not in the vocabulary are ignored.
  • Forgetting that X is a sparse matrix: use .toarray() to see the numbers.
  • Reading a cosine similarity of 0.3 as "30% the same text". It's a score for comparing, not a percentage.

✅ Recap

  • The vocabulary is every different word in the corpus; a vector is a list of numbers, one per word.
  • Bag-of-words: count each word, ignore the order. CountVectorizer().fit_transform(docs).
  • get_feature_names_out() lists the words; .toarray() shows the numbers.
  • stop_words="english" removes common words; ngram_range=(1, 2) adds word pairs.
  • TF-IDF = frequent here × rare elsewhere. TfidfVectorizer gives these scores.
  • cosine_similarity scores how alike two vectors are (0 to 1); the highest score is the best match.
