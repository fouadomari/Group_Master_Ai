🤔 Why this matters

Is this e-mail spam? Will this customer leave? Is this transaction fraud? Most real machine-learning jobs are classification: putting each example into one of a few classes (groups). On Day 16 you trained a classifier and got answers. Today you'll also get the model's confidence, because "spam, 51% sure" and "spam, 99% sure" deserve different treatment.

Think of a weather forecast: it doesn't say "rain" or "no rain", it says "70% chance of rain", and you decide whether that's enough to take an umbrella. That decision point is the threshold.

📈 Logistic regression: a line that gives probabilities

Despite the word "regression" in its name, logistic regression is a classification model. It works in two steps:

  1. Like yesterday's linear regression, it adds up the features times their slopes, which gives a score that can be any number.
  2. It squeezes that score into a probability between 0 and 1 with an S-shaped curve: very negative scores become almost 0, very positive ones almost 1.

Each row adds up to 1. The first sample is 93% benign: the model is quite sure. The third is 55% malignant: a coin flip, and in fact the model got it wrong. Probabilities tell you which answers to trust.

🎚️ The decision threshold

predict simply says "benign" when the chance of benign is at least 0.5. That cut-off is the threshold, and you can choose a different one. Suppose a mistaken "benign" is much worse than a mistaken "malignant" (which would just lead to more tests). Then we might only say "benign" when the model is at least 90% sure:

Shift + Enter

[:, 1] means "all rows, column 1" (Day 9), and & means "and" for arrays (Day 11). A higher threshold makes fewer "benign" calls, but those it makes are much safer. Choosing the threshold isn't a maths question; it depends on what each kind of mistake costs. You'll measure this properly on Day 20.

🏘️ K-nearest neighbours: ask the neighbours

On Day 16 you used KNN without looking inside. Its idea: to classify a new sample, find the k training samples that are closest to it (most similar measurements) and let them vote. There's no formula to learn; "fitting" just means remembering the training data.

Here's one made-up new sample and its 5 nearest neighbours:

Shift + Enter

Four neighbours say benign (1), one says malignant (0), so the answer is benign, and KNN's "probability" is simply the share of votes: 4 out of 5 = 0.8.

Choosing k: with k=1 a single odd neighbour decides everything (too nervous). With a huge k the vote includes samples that aren't similar at all (too lazy). And k can't be bigger than the number of training samples:

Shift + Enter

Read the last line: Expected n_neighbors <= n_samples_fit. You can't ask 500 neighbours when only 426 exist.

⚖️ Why features must be on the same scale

"Closest" is measured as a distance, by adding up the differences in every feature. Now remember the sizes: a difference in area can be hundreds, a difference in smoothness is about 0.01. So KNN on all 30 raw features basically only looks at area and ignores the rest. It's like comparing people by their salary in cents and their height in kilometres.

The fix is scaling: rewrite each feature so that it has mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (Day 9: std measures the typical spread). Then a "big difference" means the same for every feature. StandardScaler does it:

Shift + Enter

Fit the scaler on the training set only. The test set plays the role of future data you haven't seen yet. If its numbers helped decide the mean and std, a little of the "exam" would leak into the "practice", and your score would be too optimistic.

🔗 Pipelines: scaling and model in one

Remembering to scale the training set, then the test set, then every new sample, is easy to get wrong. A pipeline glues the steps together into one model. When you call fit, it fits the scaler and then the model on the training data; when you call predict or score, it scales the new data first, automatically:

⚠️ Common mistakes

  • Thinking logistic regression predicts numbers. It's a classifier that gives probabilities.
  • Forgetting that predict_proba has one column per class: [:, 1] is the chance of class 1.
  • Using KNN (or logistic regression) on features with very different scales. Scale first.
  • Fitting the scaler on all the data, or on the test set. Fit it on the training set only (a pipeline does this for you).
  • Choosing n_neighbors bigger than the training set → ValueError.
  • Comparing models that were tested on different splits.

✅ Recap

  • LogisticRegression: a weighted sum squeezed into a probability. predict_proba gives the chances, predict uses a 0.5 threshold.
  • Your own threshold: model.predict_proba(X)[:, 1] >= 0.9.
  • KNN: the k closest training samples vote. Small k = nervous, big k = lazy.
  • StandardScaler gives every feature mean 0 and std 1. Fit on the training set only.
  • make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), model) scales and models in one step.
  • Compare models on the same split, with a baseline.
