🤔 Why this matters

Yesterday's perceptron rule only works for a single neuron with a step. Real networks have millions of weights, and they all learn with the same simple idea: gradient descent.

Imagine you are on a hillside in thick fog and want to reach the bottom of the valley. You can't see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a small step downhill, feel again, take another step… and eventually you arrive. In machine learning, the "height" is how wrong the model is, and the "position" is the values of its weights. Many of the models you trained with .fit() in the last week, and every chatbot, learn this way.

📏 Measuring "how wrong": the loss

Before a model can improve, it needs one number that says how wrong it is. That number is called the loss. Smaller is better; 0 means perfect. A very common loss is the mean squared error (MSE): take each error (prediction − truth), square it so negatives don't cancel positives and big mistakes count extra, then take the average.

📐 The slope tells you which way is downhill

At any w, the ground has a slope: how much the loss goes up when w grows a tiny bit.

  • Slope positive → the loss rises to the right → step left (make w smaller).
  • Slope negative → the loss falls to the right → step right (make w bigger).
  • Slope near 0 → you're at the bottom.

The slope is also called the gradient (with many weights, the gradient is the list of slopes, one per weight). You can measure it by nudging w a tiny bit and seeing how the loss changes. Calculus gives a formula that does the same thing exactly; for our MSE it is mean(2 × error × cups). You don't need the calculus, just the idea:

Shift + Enter

Both ways agree. At w = 1 the slope is negative (go right), at w = 5 it is positive (go left), and at w = 3 it is almost 0: we're nearly at the bottom.

🚶 Gradient descent: step downhill, repeat

The whole algorithm is one line, repeated:

w = w − learning_rate × slope

The minus sign makes us go against the slope, which is downhill. The learning rate (from yesterday) sets the step size. Let's start from a bad guess, w = 0:

Shift + Enter

w walks towards about 3.0 (a cup costs about 3) and the loss shrinks with every step. Notice the steps get smaller near the bottom, because the slope gets flatter.

Choosing the learning rate

Too small and you crawl. Too big and you jump right over the valley, landing higher on the other side each time. Let's watch w during 20 steps with three learning rates:

Shift + Enter
  • lr = 0.002: safe but slow, still far from 3 after 20 steps.
  • lr = 0.03: gets there in a few steps.
  • lr = 0.1: zigzags further away each step and shoots off the chart. This is called diverging.

Picking the learning rate is a matter of trying a few values, and it is one of the most important settings in deep learning.

🧱 A 2-layer network

Yesterday one neuron failed at XOR, because it can only draw one straight line. The fix is to put a layer of neurons in between:

  • Input layer: the 2 inputs (no calculation).
  • Hidden layer: 4 neurons. Each looks at both inputs and draws its own line. "Hidden" because we never see its answers directly.
  • Output layer: 1 neuron that combines the 4 hidden answers into the final answer.

Instead of a weight list per neuron, a whole layer's weights go in one 2D array (a matrix). W1 has shape (2, 4): 2 inputs × 4 hidden neurons, one column per neuron. So X @ W1 computes all 4 neurons for all 4 examples at once. We start with small random weights (seeded, so you get the same numbers), and every neuron uses the sigmoid. 🔙 Backpropagation: passing the blame backwards

To use gradient descent we need the slope of the loss for every weight. The trick for getting them all is called backpropagation ("backprop"). Think of a group project that got a bad grade: the final presenter (the output neuron) knows how wrong the result was. It passes the blame back to the team members (hidden neurons), each in proportion to how much it listened to them (its weights). Then everyone adjusts a little.

One new tool first. .T transposes an array: rows become columns. Backprop needs it to send things the opposite way through a layer:

Shift + Enter

Here is one complete training step. Read the comments line by line:

  1. Forward pass: compute H and out.
  2. Output blame: the error out − y, times out × (1 − out). That second part is the sigmoid's own slope: a neuron that is already very sure (near 0 or 1) changes little.
  3. Gradient for W2: blame × the hidden answers that fed in, like yesterday's "error × input".
  4. Hidden blame: send the output blame back through W2, then times each hidden neuron's sigmoid slope.
  5. Gradient for W1: hidden blame × the inputs.
  6. Update every weight and bias: W = W − lr × gradient.
Shift + Enter

Small print: the exact MSE slope also has a factor 2 and a division by the number of examples. We leave them out: they only change the step size, and the learning rate already controls that.

The loss went down a little. Now repeat the same step 5000 times (5000 epochs, since each step uses all 4 examples). It takes a second or two:

🎉 XOR solved! The outputs are close to 0, 1, 1, 0. The hidden neurons each learned their own line, and the output neuron learned how to combine them. The loss curve shows the learning:

The loss drops fast during the first few hundred epochs, then flattens out near 0: after that the network is only polishing its answers. Loss curves like this are the first thing deep learning engineers look at when they train a model.

⚠️ Common mistakes

  • Adding the slope instead of subtracting it: w + lr * slope walks uphill and the loss grows.
  • A learning rate that is too big: the loss jumps around or explodes. Try 10 times smaller.
  • A learning rate that is too small: nothing seems to happen. Try 10 times bigger, or more steps.
  • Shape mismatches between layers. Print the .shape of every array.
  • Starting all weights at exactly 0 in a network: every hidden neuron then learns the same thing. Use small random numbers.

✅ Recap

  • The loss is one number saying how wrong the model is. MSE = mean of squared errors.
  • The slope (gradient) says which way the loss goes up; we step the other way.
  • Gradient descent: w = w − lr × slope, repeated many times.
  • The learning rate must be not too small (slow) and not too big (diverges).
  • A 2-layer network: H = sigmoid(X @ W1 + b1), out = sigmoid(H @ W2 + b2).
  • Backpropagation passes the blame from the output back through the layers to get every weight's slope.
