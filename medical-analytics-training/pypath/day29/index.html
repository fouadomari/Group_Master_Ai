🤔 Why this matters

Sentiment is the feeling behind a text: happy, unhappy, or somewhere in between. A shop with 50 000 reviews can't read them all, but a sentiment classifier can sort them in a second and point out the angry ones. The same idea sorts emails into spam and not spam, or support tickets into topics.

It's supervised learning (Day 16): we give the model reviews together with the right answer (the label), and it learns which words go with which label.

🧠 Which words does the model rely on?

Logistic regression learns one weight (number) per vocabulary word, stored in coef_. A word with a big positive weight pushes a review towards "positive" (the second class in classes_); a big negative weight pushes it towards "negative". named_steps lets us take the two parts out of the pipeline:

Shift + Enter

Most of it makes sense: great, excellent, love on one side; poor, terrible, awful on the other. But look: the and not count as negative words! In our small dataset, unhappy customers simply happened to write "the" and "not" more often. The model doesn't understand English: it finds words that go together with a label. Reading the weights is a great way to catch such silly lessons.

🎲 A second opinion: Naive Bayes

Naive Bayes is a classic text classifier. It looks at how often each word appears in positive and in negative training reviews, and combines those chances for a new review. It's called "naive" because it treats every word as if it had nothing to do with its neighbours. It is very fast and works surprisingly well on text. scikit-learn's version for word counts is MultinomialNB; swapping it in is one line:

🙃 The "not bad" problem

A bag of words has no idea about word order. "not bad" is just not (a negative word, as we saw) plus bad (also negative):

Shift + Enter

{text:26} pads the text to 26 characters so the arrows line up.

"not bad" comes out negative: wrong. Yesterday's idea, bigrams (ngram_range=(1, 2)), lets the model see "not bad" as one feature. Let's measure whether it helps here:

Shift + Enter

Surprise: on our data, bigrams make things worse. To learn that "not bad" is positive, the model must see "not bad" in positive training reviews, and our 60 training reviews contain it zero times. Meanwhile the extra word-pair columns, most of them seen only once, add noise. With thousands of reviews, bigrams usually do help. The lesson is the one from Day 20: don't guess, measure.

🚧 Limits

Our classifier is useful, but know what it can't do:

  • Sarcasm: "Great, it broke after one day. Just great." contains great twice.
  • Tiny data: 80 made-up reviews can't cover the words real customers use. Real systems train on many thousands.
  • New topics: a model trained on product reviews may fail on film or restaurant reviews, where words like "cheap" or "long" mean something else.
  • Mixed feelings: "lovely design, terrible battery" doesn't fit into two boxes.
Shift + Enter

Modern language models (like the ones behind chatbots) read whole sentences in order and handle many of these cases much better. Tomorrow's "What next" section points the way.

⚠️ Common mistakes

  • Calling predict before fit → NotFittedError.
  • Passing one string instead of a list: use model.predict(["some text"]).
  • Testing on the training reviews. The score looks great but means nothing; always keep a test set.
  • Reading the predict_proba columns in the wrong order: check model.classes_.
  • Believing a small difference in accuracy on a small test set. One review can change it by 5%.

✅ Recap

  • Sentiment analysis = text classification with labels like positive / negative.
  • make_pipeline(TfidfVectorizer(), LogisticRegression()) goes from raw text to a label.
  • Split with stratify, fit on training text, score on test text.
  • coef_ + get_feature_names_out() show which words push towards each label.
  • MultinomialNB (Naive Bayes) is a fast classic alternative.
  • Bags of words miss order ("not bad") and sarcasm; bigrams can help, but only with enough data. Measure!
